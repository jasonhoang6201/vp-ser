Is Text All You Need? Text as a Universal Information
Bottleneck for Speech LLMs
Ming-Hao Hsu1,†
Yuxuan Hu2
Shujie Liu3,∗
Jinyu Li2
Yan Lu3
Zhizheng Wu1,∗
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) provide a powerful reasoning backbone for speech
understanding, but integrating continuous acoustic signals into a pretrained LLM
remains challenging. A central design question is how to tie speech representations
to the LLM’s text input space while preserving the continuous variation needed for
different speech tasks. In this work, we propose Convex Gate (C-Gate), a speech–
LLM bridge that represents each frame as a convex combination of frozen token
embeddings. This architectural convex-hull constraint fixes the output codebook
while allowing continuous mixtures learned from task supervision. With a Whisper-
large-v3 encoder, a partially adapted Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct decoder, and joint
training on speech recognition, emotion recognition, and spoken reasoning, C-
Gate performs accurate transcription and emotion recognition, our representative
semantic and acoustic tasks, through one shared decoder. On text-only benchmarks,
the adapted decoder loses instruction-following and MMLU accuracy and matches
or exceeds the original model on GSM8K and HellaSwag. We further design
analyses and interventions on the trained model to examine where the bridge
sequence carries task information and how the decoder reads it. These findings
show how fixed text embeddings can support both semantic and acoustic readouts
through continuous speech-conditioned mixtures.
1
Introduction
Reusing pretrained large language models as the reasoning backbone for speech understanding is
increasingly necessary for scalable multimodal systems. Instruction-tuned LLMs such as Qwen2.5-
7B-Instruct [Yang et al., 2024] carry world knowledge, calibrated answer behavior, and tool-use
priors whose retraining from speech-aligned data alone would be prohibitively expensive. A standard
design pattern attaches a speech encoder to a pretrained LLM via a learned bridge [Li et al., 2023,
Rubenstein et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023, Tang et al., 2024, Chu et al., 2023, 2024], allowing a
single decoder to serve transcription, paralinguistic, and spoken-reasoning workloads. Under this
paradigm, the central design question is what representation form should be used to feed speech
information into the LLM context. One answer is continuous prompting, which conditions pretrained
LLMs through learned prefix representations [Li and Liang, 2021, Lester et al., 2021]. Another,
increasingly popular, answer constructs speech representations from the LLM’s own input embedding
table [Ma et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025].
Existing interfaces make different choices about vocabulary alignment and continuous expressivity.
Some methods align speech representations with discrete tokens using objectives such as CTC or text
alignment [Ma et al., 2025, Fan et al., 2025, Peng et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026a]. These objectives
emphasize semantic content, while emotion recognition also requires acoustic cues such as prosody.
Other approaches learn continuous tokens or latent embeddings without restricting them to mixtures
of vocabulary embeddings [Tang et al., 2024, Chu et al., 2023, 2024]. This gives the bridge flexibility

to choose its output representations. Together, these designs motivate separating two choices: the
embedding vectors available to the bridge and the supervision used to combine them. We ask whether
a fixed text embedding table can support both semantic and acoustic targets when speech selects
continuous mixtures through task supervision. We take speech recognition and emotion recognition
as the representative semantic and acoustic tasks.
To study this question, we propose the Convex Gate (C-Gate), a speech–LLM bridge with an
architectural convex-hull constraint. We represent each time step as a convex combination of LLM
input embeddings, fixing the output codebook while preserving continuous expressivity. Concretely,
C-Gate scores the full vocabulary using downsampled speech features, selects the top-16 entries, and
renormalizes their weights to combine rows of the frozen embedding table E. The bridge uses the
raw embedding values, with no value projection or post-codebook multilayer perceptron (MLP). This
construction separates a fixed output geometry from the choice of routing supervision. Within this
construction, task losses train only the selection and the mixture weights, allowing the support to
carry information useful for transcription or emotion recognition. The shared embedding coordinates
also make the selected tokens, mixture weights, and decoder attention directly inspectable.
Our experiments show that C-Gate supports both transcription and emotion recognition with a shared
decoder. Beyond task performance, we find that selected token identities carry emotion information
even when their mixture weights are nearly uniform. Interventions on trained models further show
that decoding depends on temporal order and the learned key–value correspondence. These findings
distinguish the information recoverable from the selected supports from how the decoder uses the full
bridge sequence.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
• Geometry-Constrained Speech–LLM Interface: We introduce C-Gate, which represents each
speech frame as a sparse convex mixture of 16 rows of the frozen LLM embedding table, selected
by scoring the full vocabulary. The bridge applies no projection after the embedding table, so its
outputs stay inside the convex hull of the text embeddings, and within the bridge the task losses
train only which rows are selected and how they are weighted.
• Semantic and Acoustic Tasks from One Frozen Codebook: We show that convex mixtures of a
frozen text embedding table support transcription as a semantic task and emotion recognition as
an acoustic task with a shared, partially adapted decoder. In our experiments, a single checkpoint
trained jointly on speech recognition, emotion recognition, and spoken reasoning reaches 3.98%
and 8.42% word error rate (WER) on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other and 84.3% accuracy on
emotion test recordings from eight corpora.
• Representation and Readout Analysis: We show that the mixture weights are nearly uniform and
that the identities of the selected tokens alone support a linear emotion readout at least as accurate
as that of the pooled encoder states. Interventions on the trained model show that decoding depends
on frame order and on the learned key–value correspondence, while setting the weights to exactly
uniform has a much smaller effect.

Related Work
Speech-to-LLM Adapters.
Speech–LLM systems require an adapter that converts acoustic encoder
features into a sequence of embeddings the language-model decoder can consume. Adapter designs
differ in how they aggregate acoustic features over time and construct the representations passed
to the decoder [Tang et al., 2024, Ma et al., 2024a, Lee and Chun, 2026]. One line of designs uses
query-based aggregation, which has a precedent in vision–language modeling [Alayrac et al., 2022],
where BLIP-2 introduced the Q-Former to connect a frozen image encoder and a frozen language
model [Li et al., 2023]. SALMONN extends this design to speech and audio through a window-level
Q-Former that produces a variable-length sequence of audio embeddings [Li et al., 2023, Tang et al.,
2024]. Qwen-Audio and Qwen2-Audio, in turn, connect audio encoders to LLMs through audio–text
pretraining across multiple tasks [Chu et al., 2023, 2024]. However, these adapters leave the bridge a
free output space, so where its outputs settle relative to the text embeddings is an outcome of training
[Hsu et al., 2026a] rather than a property of the design. We instead fix that geometry by construction
and ask what a bridge confined to the text embedding hull can still carry.

Vocabulary Mixtures and Alignment Objectives.
Beyond the adapter architecture, a speech–LLM
interface is shaped by both its output representation and the supervision used to learn the mapping
[Wang et al., 2023, Deng et al., 2025, Ma et al., 2025]. The output representation can also be
discrete, and codec-based tokenizers such as SpeechTokenizer show that discrete speech units can
retain speaker and prosodic information alongside content [Zhang et al., 2024, Mousavi et al., 2024,
Hsu et al., 2025]. Alignment can guide temporal aggregation, as in SSR’s segmentation of speech
features and AlignFormer’s use of CTC alignments to define dynamic Q-Former windows [Tan et al.,
2025, Fan et al., 2025]. CTC posteriors can also provide a common interface for speech and text
[Ma et al., 2025], which TASU exploits by simulating these posteriors from text-only data during
alignment training [Peng et al., 2026]. Behavioral supervision offers another route [Cuervo et al.,
2025]: BLSP learns from text continuations, while TARS uses reinforcement learning to align speech-
and text-conditioned hidden representations and generated outputs [Wang et al., 2023, 2026a]. Yet
in each case the tie between speech and text enters through the training signal, and a transcript or a
text-conditioned behavior defines what the speech representation should match. By contrast, C-Gate
places the tie in the architecture and trains the bridge with the task losses alone, so the targets decide
what the mixture keeps.
A direct connection to the LLM vocabulary can also be built into the output representation by
constructing mixtures of token embeddings [Ma et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025]. LegoSLM uses CTC
posteriors as mixture weights over LLM embeddings, with an additional blank representation, to
reconstruct speech embeddings [Ma et al., 2025]. For automatic speech recognition (ASR), Yang
et al. [2025] softly discretize audio using a codebook initialized from the LLM embedding table,
studying sparse mixtures with both updated and fixed codebooks. These methods establish vocabulary
mixtures as a speech–LLM interface for transcription, where the target is itself a token sequence and
a CTC posterior or an ASR-trained discretization supplies the weights [Ma et al., 2025, Yang et al.,
2025]. They leave open, however, whether the original frozen table can also carry an acoustic target
that no transcript token names, with weights learned from the task losses alone. C-Gate takes up
this question: each bridge output is a normalized mixture over selected rows of the original, frozen
embedding table, and we learn the mixture weights from task losses without CTC supervision.
Evaluation beyond Transcription.
Transcription accuracy alone does not reveal how well a speech–
LLM interface supports paralinguistic understanding and spoken reasoning [Ma et al., 2024b, Wang
et al., 2024a, 2026b,c, Hsu et al., 2026b]. VoiceBench evaluates responses to spoken instructions
[Chen et al., 2026], and SpeechMMLU probes knowledge-based question answering from speech [Tan
et al., 2025]. MMAU extends evaluation to understanding and reasoning across speech, environmental
sounds, and music [Sakshi et al., 2025]. Interpreting these scores also requires distinguishing correct
answers from answers that depend on the audio input [Rouditchenko et al., 2025, Zang et al., 2025,
He et al., 2026]. Work on audio contribution examines this distinction directly [He et al., 2026], and
studies of multiple-choice evaluation show that scores can vary with the ordering and wording of
answer choices [López et al., 2025]. These controls ask whether a benchmark score depends on the
audio. Beyond adopting them for the reasoning benchmarks, we extend the same logic to the bridge
and intervene on the waveform, the key–value mapping, and the frame order of a trained checkpoint
to locate what the decoder reads for transcription and emotion recognition.

4
Experimental Setup
This section describes the model configuration, the training data and optimization, and the evaluation
benchmarks. More details can be found in Appendices A and B.
4.1
Model Configuration
We initialize the acoustic encoder with pretrained Whisper-large-v3 [Radford et al., 2023] and the
autoregressive decoder with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [Yang et al., 2024]. Following the notation in
Section 3, the encoder produces acoustic features ht with encoder dimension denc = 1,280. The
decoder’s input embedding table E has embedding dimension dllm = 3,584 and V = 152,064 rows.
The C-Gate scorer maps acoustic features and vocabulary embeddings into queries qt and keys K
with a shared scoring dimension dp = 512. We set the support size k to 16 and the mean-pooling
stride r to 4.
During adaptation, we train the C-Gate scorer and the query, key, value, and output attention projec-
tions in decoder layers 0–23. These are 2.5M scorer parameters and 704.8M attention parameters,
which together make up 8.6% of the 8.26B parameters of the full model. All remaining parameters
are frozen, including the Whisper-large-v3 encoder, the input embeddings, MLPs, normalization
layers, language-model head, and decoder layers 24–27. The embedding values mixed by C-Gate
therefore remain fixed throughout training.
4.2
Training Data and Optimization
ASR.
We train on the 960 hours of LibriSpeech [Panayotov et al., 2015].
Emotion Recognition.
We pool the English portion of CAMEO [Christop and Czajka, 2025],
which includes CREMA-D, EMNS, eNTERFACE, JL-Corpus, and RAVDESS, with ESD English
[Zhou et al., 2021], IEMOCAP [Busso et al., 2008], SAVEE [Jackson and Haq, 2014], TESS [Dupuis
and Pichora-Fuller, 2011], and MELD audio [Poria et al., 2019], and map their labels to eight classes.
We split each corpus by recording into a training and a test portion. We train on 53,246 recordings,
and the emotion test split has 2,063 recordings from CREMA-D, EMNS, eNTERFACE, JL-Corpus,
ESD, IEMOCAP, SAVEE, and TESS.
Reasoning.
We use question-answer corpora with synthesized spoken questions, including ARC
[Clark et al., 2018], GSM8K [Cobbe et al., 2021], MATH [Hendrycks et al., 2021b], and MMLU-Pro
[Wang et al., 2024b]. Appendix A lists all sources and their sizes.
Training Configurations.
C-Gate denotes the three-task model trained jointly on ASR, emotion
recognition, and reasoning. The other training-task combinations are training-data ablations: a
two-task model trained on ASR and emotion, and single-task models trained on ASR, emotion, or
reasoning alone. All configurations share the bridge, the adaptation scope, the learning rates, and the
batch size. Appendix A lists the training length and learning-rate schedule of each configuration.
Optimization.
We optimize with AdamW at learning rates of 10−4 for the bridge and 2 × 10−5
for decoder attention, a linear warm-up over 5,000 updates, and an effective batch size of 256.
Appendix A provides the remaining settings.

Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics
Decoding is greedy throughout, and Appendix B gives the prompts, output limits, scoring rules, and
the construction of spoken BBH.
ASR.
We report WER on the LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other splits, which contain 2,620 and
2,939 utterances.
Emotion Recognition.
We report the accuracy of the generated completion on our test recordings
of ESD, IEMOCAP, and CREMA-D, the three corpora with the most test recordings, and give the
other corpora in Appendix C. The analyses in Section 6 use the whole emotion test split.
Reasoning.
We report answer accuracy on spoken BBH [Suzgun et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2026],
SpeechMMLU (SpMMLU) [Tan et al., 2025], and MMAU [Sakshi et al., 2025]. In these three
benchmarks the text prompt contains the written question, and the audio carries the spoken question
or the audio clip that the question refers to.
Text-Only Capabilities.
To assess text-only capabilities after speech adaptation, we compare C-
Gate with the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model. Examples, prompts, shot counts, decoding settings,
parsers, and seeds are matched within each benchmark. We report prompt-level strict IFEval accuracy
[Zhou et al., 2023] and 0-shot normalized HellaSwag accuracy [Zellers et al., 2019].

Main Results
We report the experimental results in terms of word error rate (LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other)
and accuracy (emotion recognition and reasoning benchmarks) in Table 1. Appendix C provides
additional results, including the LibriSpeech WERs of published speech LLMs and emotion accuracy
for every corpus and by class.
5.1
Overall Task Performance
Semantic and Acoustic Tasks.
We evaluate ASR and emotion recognition as the representative
semantic and acoustic tasks. C-Gate, trained jointly on ASR, emotion recognition, and reasoning (last
row of Table 1), attains 3.98% and 8.42% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other and emotion
accuracy of 94.7% on ESD, 64.6% on IEMOCAP, and 74.2% on CREMA-D. In both cases, one
decoder generates the transcripts and the emotion labels from mixtures of 16 frozen token embeddings
per frame. This shows that continuous mixtures of fixed text embeddings support a semantic and an
acoustic task under partial decoder adaptation.
Effect of Joint Training.
We compare C-Gate with models trained on subsets of the three tasks.
For ASR, the WER of the joint models stays within 0.55 points of the ASR-only model on test-clean
and 1.03 points on test-other. For emotion recognition, the three models trained on emotion data
agree within 1.3 points on ESD, IEMOCAP, and CREMA-D, and their accuracy over the whole
emotion test split is 84.0%, 84.1%, and 84.3%. The jointly trained models therefore score close to
the single-task models on both tasks, and Section 6 examines how the bridge sequence carries both.

5.2
Text-Only Performance after Speech Adaptation
To examine how speech adaptation changes the decoder’s text-only capabilities, we compare C-Gate
with the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model under matched evaluation settings (Table 2). IFEval
[Zhou et al., 2023] prompt-level strict accuracy decreases by 12.01 percentage points after speech

The input embedding table stays fixed, so both changes come from the adapted attention projections.

Representation and Readout Analysis
We ask where the bridge sequence carries task information and how the decoder reads it. We examine
the mixture weights, the selected tokens, and the decoder’s attention to the audio frames, and then
intervene on the bridge at fixed model weights. All interventions use the emotion test split and the
first 500 test-clean utterances, on which the unmodified C-Gate attains 84.29% emotion accuracy and
4.01% WER.
6.1
Mixture Concentration
We first examine how much task information the mixture weights carry. The renormalized mixture
weights are close to uniform in every training configuration and for every input task (Table 3).
Normalized entropy ranges from 0.960 to 0.990, and the bridge vectors of C-Gate have a mean
cosine similarity of 0.94 to 0.95 with the unweighted mean of their 16 selected embeddings. To
measure what this residual variation contributes, we set every weight to exactly 1/16 on the same
selected tokens, which lowers emotion accuracy to 81.63% and raises WER to 5.85% (Table 17 in
Appendix E). Appendix D shows that the softmax before truncation is nearly flat as well and that
near-uniform weights still pass gradient to the scorer. The weights therefore carry a small part of the

6.2
Emotion Probes across Representations
We next test whether the identities of the selected tokens alone carry emotion information. To this
end, we train logistic-regression probes on the emotion test split, with speaker-disjoint folds repeated
over ten random partitions (Table 15 in Appendix D). A bag-of-tokens probe sees only how often
each of the 1,000 most frequently selected tokens occurs in a recording’s supports, without weights
or frame order. It reaches 63.3% accuracy, against 57.6% for mean-pooled Whisper states and 66.8%
for mean-pooled bridge vectors, and exceeds the Whisper probe in every partition. For reference, the
most frequent class accounts for 23.7% of the recordings. This readout is not lexical: a label word
appears in only 0.08% of the supports, and the most frequent tokens are unrelated word pieces that
occur in nearly every recording. This shows that the selected token identities alone support a linear
emotion readout at least as accurate as that of the pooled encoder states.
6.3
Task-Conditional Attention
We then examine how the decoder attends to the audio frames under each task. For each example, we
feed the evaluation prompt, the bridge frames, and the reference answer, and take the attention from the
first five answer tokens to the audio frames at decoder layer 14, averaged over heads. We summarize
this matrix by its entropy-based effective rank [Roy and Vetterli, 2007], exp(−P
j pj log pj) with
pj = σj/ P
i σi for singular values σj, which is at most five. A low rank means that the answer
tokens share one attention pattern over the audio, and a rank near five means that each token attends
to different frames. When transcribing, the three models trained on ASR reach ranks of 4.43 to 4.47,
while the emotion-only model stays at 2.80 on the same inputs (Fig. 2). For emotion completion, the
two joint models have ranks of 2.87 and 2.71. We report layer 14 because layers 14 and 15 are where
the transcription rank peaks, and Appendix D summarizes the other layers and the full-transcript
ranks. The joint models thus show position-specific attention when transcribing and more shared
attention over the emotion answer.

Key–Value Mapping.
We perturb keys or values inside C-Gate while prompt embeddings and the
language-model head stay unchanged. Permuting the values, replacing them with Gaussian vectors, or
permuting the keys lowers emotion accuracy to 10–13% and raises WER above 97% (Fig. 3). These
conditions differ in what they preserve: a row permutation keeps the set of embedding vectors and
hence its convex hull, conv(PE) = conv(E) for a permutation matrix P, while Gaussian substitution
additionally changes the embedding geometry. The permutation controls therefore remove only the
learned correspondence between a score and the vector it retrieves, and the outputs depend on that
correspondence beyond membership in the hull.

We permute the bridge frames within non-overlapping windows of increasing
size. Averaged over three permutation seeds, a full shuffle lowers emotion accuracy to 52.25% and
raises WER to 187.52% (Fig. 4). Transcription fails once local order is lost, with 51% WER at a
window of four frames, while emotion accuracy degrades gradually and stays far above the most
frequent class under a full shuffle, in line with the order-free probe of Section 6.2.

Support Size and Pooling Stride
Finally, we vary the support size k and the pooling stride at inference in the C-Gate checkpoint trained
with k = 16 and stride 4, without retraining (Fig. 5). Small supports break both tasks: k = 1 raises
WER to 337.10% and lowers emotion accuracy to 55.89%. Around the trained setting, by contrast,
the changes are small. At k = 8 emotion accuracy is unchanged at 84.54% and WER rises to 5.18%,
and at k = 32 WER is unchanged at 3.89% and emotion accuracy falls to 82.65%, where unchanged
means that the paired 95% interval includes zero (Appendix E). At k = 64 emotion accuracy falls to
79.11% and WER rises to 4.84%. The stride behaves similarly: changing it from 4 to 2 or 8 degrades
both scores, to 14.07% and 36.50% WER and to 81.63% and 70.29% emotion accuracy (Appendix E).
For this checkpoint, both tasks therefore depend on mixing several embeddings per frame and on the
trained stride.
7
Discussion and Conclusion
We propose C-Gate, a speech–LLM bridge that represents each speech frame as a sparse convex
mixture of frozen text embeddings. The mixture construction fixes the available embedding values,
while task supervision learns which values to select and combine. With a partially adapted decoder,
C-Gate supports both transcription and emotion recognition from one frozen embedding table. Our
analysis shows that the mixture weights stay near uniform, and replacing them with exactly uniform
weights costs 2.7 points of emotion accuracy and 1.8 points of WER, so the speech-dependent
variation lies mainly in which tokens are selected and in their order. In particular, token identities
alone support a linear emotion readout at least as accurate as that of the pooled encoder states, and
the decoder’s outputs degrade when frame order or the key–value correspondence is disturbed.
Limitations and Future Work.
Our study uses one encoder–LLM pair and one training run per
configuration. Matched ablations of the convex-hull constraint and of attention adaptation are left to
future work, and Appendix G summarizes the evidence behind each design choice. On the evaluation
side, speech adaptation lowers IFEval and MMLU accuracy, and the reasoning scores depend little
on the audio in the tested diagnostic. Finally, scoring the full vocabulary takes about 2.9 × 1010
multiply–accumulate operations per 30-second input (Appendix F), and shortlist scoring is left to
future work.
Implications and Broader Impact.
Fixed embedding mixtures provide an inspectable interface for
studying how language models use acoustic information. Joint speech understanding could support
accessible voice interfaces, while emotion inference from acted speech can be unreliable in natural
settings and can enable intrusive speaker profiling. More broadly, speech quality, text retention, and
audio contribution are best evaluated together.

Table 5 gives the evaluated checkpoint of each configuration. All configurations share the bridge, the

A.4
Optimization
We use AdamW with coefficients (0.9, 0.999), weight decay 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The
learning rate is 10−4 for the bridge and 2 × 10−5 for decoder attention, with a linear warm-up
over 5,000 optimizer updates followed by a cosine schedule whose horizon is 300,000 optimizer
updates for the joint configurations and 100,000 for the single-task configurations. Each training-loop
iteration processes four micro-batches of two examples on each of eight GPUs, and the optimizer
is updated every four iterations, which gives the effective batch size of 256. Training uses bfloat16
mixed precision. The data sampler is seeded.

B.1
ASR
The instruction is “Transcribe the following speech.” Decoding is greedy with at most 256 new tokens.
The prompt embeddings precede the audio embeddings. Audio is sampled at 16 kHz and truncated
to 30 seconds, which affects nine test-clean and seven test-other utterances whose full reference
transcripts stay in the denominator. We uppercase references and predictions, split on whitespace,
and divide the total number of word errors by the total number of reference words. For C-Gate,
applying Whisper’s English text normalizer instead gives 3.83% and 8.23% WER, and excluding
the truncated utterances gives 3.88% and 8.21%. The teacher-forced WER in Table 13 appends the
reference tokens to the same prompt and audio, predicts each token from its reference prefix, and
scores the decoded argmax sequence.
B.2
Emotion Recognition
The instruction is “The speaker sounds”, which matches the training target “The speaker sounds
{emotion}.” Decoding is greedy with at most 32 new tokens. We lowercase the output and take
the first whole-word occurrence of a label or alias, where the labels are angry, happy, sad, neutral,
fearful, disgusted, surprised, and calm, and the aliases are anger, happiness, sadness, fear, disgust,
and surprise. Outputs without a label count as incorrect.
B.3
Reasoning
For spoken BBH, SpeechMMLU, and MMAU, the text prompt contains the written question, with
answer options where the task has them, followed by an answer-format instruction. The audio carries
the spoken question for BBH and SpeechMMLU and the audio clip under question for MMAU.
Generation is limited to 8 tokens for yes/no, true/false, and valid/invalid BBH categories, 32 tokens
for free-form BBH categories, and 64 tokens for multiple-choice questions. Answer extraction
is category-specific for BBH and letter-based for multiple-choice questions. Our SpeechMMLU
protocol scores generated answers, whereas the benchmark’s source scores choice likelihoods [Tan
et al., 2025].
B.4
Spoken BBH
Spoken BBH combines the 1,000 questions of the four VoiceBench BBH categories, which are
hyperbaton, navigate, sports understanding, and web of lies, with 3,392 questions from 18 further
BBH categories [Suzgun et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2026]. For the further categories, we take the
question text and reference answers from the BBH release, filter for questions that can be read aloud,
and synthesize the audio with Edge TTS using the en-US-AriaNeural voice. Accuracy and parse
rate weight the 4,392 questions equally.
B.5
Text-Only Benchmarks
The base and adapted models are evaluated through the text path of the decoder with lm-evaluation-
harness 0.4.5 [EleutherAI, 2024]. Examples, chat template, shot counts, decoding, parsers, and seeds
are identical for the two models.

E.4
Support Size and Stride
Tables 19 and 20 change the support size or the pooling stride at inference for the checkpoint trained
with k = 16 and stride 4. The intervals are paired percentile bootstrap intervals from 10,000 resamples
of the 132 speakers of the emotion test split or the 9 LibriSpeech speakers, without adjustment for
multiple comparisons. A stride of 2 doubles the number of bridge frames and a stride of 8 halves it,
so the stride conditions change the sequence length as well as the temporal resolution.
F
Cost of Full-Vocabulary Scoring
This appendix counts the operations of the bridge. C-Gate scores every vocabulary entry for every
frame before selecting k entries, so selection does not reduce the scoring cost. With V = 152,064
embedding rows, dllm = 3,584, dp = 512, and T ′ = 375 frames for a 30-second input, projecting
the keys costs V dllmdp ≈2.8 × 1011 multiply–accumulate operations and scoring costs T ′V dp ≈
2.9 × 1010 per input. The T ′ × V score matrix has 5.7 × 107 entries, which occupy 109 MiB in
bfloat16 and 218 MiB in 32-bit floating point. Key projection runs once per batch during training
and can be cached at inference, because the keys depend only on fixed weights. Mixing the selected
values adds T ′kdllm operations. These counts cover the bridge alone and exclude end-to-end latency.
